# Voxel Strike

An offline voxel FPS: team deathmatch, deathmatch, gun game and zombie survival against bots, on
eight hand-authored voxel maps, with a ray traced renderer.

The map roster runs in two flavours. Five are grounded competitive layouts — a desert compound, a
logistics hall, a neon duel pit, a village and an underground facility. Three are playful and
brightly coloured: a pirate cove built around a beached galleon, a set of grass-capped islands
floating in an empty sky, and a storybook castle with a moat and painted turrets.

Everything is generated by code — maps, characters, weapons and sound. The app ships no 3D models,
no textures and no audio files, and declares no `INTERNET` permission.

- Java 17 for all app code (no Kotlin sources), C++17 through the NDK for the voxel core.
- Material 3 / Material You for every menu; dynamic colour when the device supports it.
- OpenGL ES 3.0 for rendering, with the traced passes gated on renderable float targets.

## Rendering

The pipeline is hybrid: primary visibility is rasterised, everything else is traced.

1. **G-buffer** — chunk meshes, characters, projectiles and the view model are drawn into an
   `RGBA8` target (octahedral normal, material id, baked corner occlusion), an `RG16F` screen
   space motion vector and depth. The projection carries a Halton sub-pixel jitter, while the
   motion vectors are computed from the unjittered matrices so the jitter never enters
   reprojection.
2. **Ray tracing** (`rt.frag`) — per pixel: a jittered sun shadow ray, next event estimation
   against the emissive block list, one or two cosine-weighted diffuse bounce rays (each firing
   its own shadow ray), and on Ultra a roughness-perturbed reflection ray. Results are accumulated
   by following the motion vectors into the previous frame and validating against stored linear
   depth; history weight drops as the camera accelerates.
3. **À-trous denoise** — three edge-stopping wavelet passes over the accumulated radiance.
4. **Composite** — PBR resolve: sun with GGX specular gated by traced visibility, traced indirect
   light modulated by corner occlusion, reflections through a Fresnel term, emissive, then fog.
5. **Forward** — translucent voxels (glass, grates, water), decals, particles and tracers.
6. **TAA** — the jittered frames are folded together along the motion vectors, with the history
   clamped to the neighbourhood colour box so disocclusions cannot trail.
7. **Post** — bright-pass bloom chain, ACES tone mapping, scope mask, damage and flash overlays,
   vignette, grain and a light sharpen.

### Emissive blocks are real lights

Neon strips, lamp panels, screens and lava are gathered at load time into a light list: emissive
voxels with at least one exposed face are clustered into 4³ cells, one light per cell, positioned
at the cluster's centre of mass and brightened by how many blocks it holds. Neon Arena yields 239
lights and Facility 147, well inside the 384 budget.

Every shaded point samples that list directly rather than waiting for a bounce ray to stumble
into an emitter. Candidates are drawn with resampled importance sampling — four are scored by
their unshadowed contribution, one is kept in proportion to that score, and only the survivor
pays for a shadow ray. Because next event estimation already accounts for light leaving emissive
surfaces, bounce hits deliberately drop their own emission; reflections keep it, since a mirror
should show the emitter.

### How the rays are traced

There is no hardware ray tracing API on Android, so the tracer is a DDA over the voxel volume
instead of a BVH traversal on RT cores. The whole map is one `R8UI` 3D texture; a second 3D texture
holds a per-brick Chebyshev empty-space distance (built in C++), which lets a ray jump over an
entire empty region in one step instead of walking it voxel by voxel. The effects are the real
thing — traced shadows, traced global illumination, traced reflections, temporal accumulation and
a denoiser — produced the way a voxel path tracer produces them.

### Quality presets

| Preset | Shadows | GI | Reflections | Trace resolution | Denoise | TAA |
|--------|---------|----|-------------|------------------|---------|-----|
| Low | raster only | – | – | – | – | – |
| Medium | traced | – | – | 50 % | 2 passes | yes |
| High | traced | 1 bounce | – | 50 % | 3 passes | yes |
| Ultra RT | traced, 128 steps | 2 bounces | traced | 75 % | 3 passes | yes |

Next event estimation runs on every preset that traces at all, so the emissive maps are lit
correctly even on Medium, where there are no bounce rays.

### Picture: contrast and brightness

Some maps read as too bright — Neon Arena and Facility both push their exposure well past 1. Two
tone mapper trims sit in front of that: a brightness multiplier folded into the exposure, and a
contrast applied around middle grey in log space, `0.18 * pow(color / 0.18, contrast)`, rather than
by pushing values away from 0.5. That deepens the shadows without shifting hues, and the ACES curve
that follows rolls the stretched highlights back in instead of clipping them.

Neither is touched by the quality presets — they are a look, not a cost. Settings → Graphics has
both sliders plus a one-tap high contrast preset (contrast 1.30, brightness 0.80) that toggles back
to neutral; the pause menu carries the two sliders and applies each step to the live renderer,
since the only way to judge them is against the map in front of you.

### Frame rate

A cap of 30, 60, 90, 120 or "display" is paced on the render thread: the frame schedule is kept in
absolute time and self-corrects, so one slow frame does not make the next ones early. Because
swapping already blocks on vsync the cap can only ever slow things down — asking for 30 on a 60 Hz
panel halves the rate, asking for 90 on one changes nothing. On Android 11 and up the surface also
asks the display for that rate, which is what makes 90 and 120 reachable on high refresh panels.
The cap is set in the graphics settings and in the pause menu, and the FPS counter shows what is
actually happening.

Every switch is also exposed individually in the settings screen, along with render scale and
trace resolution sliders. Devices without renderable half-float targets fall back to Low
automatically, because the traced buffers cannot store radiance or the reprojection depth there.

Low's surface scale is relative to a ~1 MP base budget (1280×800 on a 16:10 display), not to the
physical panel's density. At 75% this is 960×600 on a 2560×1600 tablet; the full-resolution Android
HUD is unaffected. Smaller panels are not enlarged. Medium/High/Ultra keep the panel-relative
scale. `RenderResolution` applies the same pixel budget to adaptive and fixed scaling without
writing any preferences. Attachment-count changes always reset the framebuffer's draw-buffer
mapping, including a two-target G-buffer becoming a single-target raster buffer.

The raster composite is compiled without RT upsampling/reflection code. Material branches only
evaluate noise that they consume; completely faded detail returns its mean before evaluating a
hash. Its 256×256 R8 noise tile is generated entirely in Java once per GL context (64 KiB), with
world-space projection and hardware bilinear interpolation. Grass, earth, brick mortar, wood
fibres and metal seams retain their material-specific shading; the cached noise repeats and is
8-bit quantised rather than being a bit-exact match to the high-quality analytic hash. Traced
presets retain that analytic hash. No per-frame noise generation or external texture assets.

`FullFramePerformanceTest` exercises a complete frame on a real GLES pbuffer and waits with
`glFinish()` only inside the test. It reports median/p95 frame time and isolated stage cost.
`PresentedFramePerformanceTest` additionally measures the actual SurfaceView/vsync/Android HUD
and running simulation using the device's saved settings. Neither performance test asserts a
device-dependent FPS target or changes graphics preferences. Diagnostic results are kept in
`docs/performance/2026-10-02.json`.

### Chunk pipeline performance

`ChunkCuller` restricts its broad phase to grid cells within the camera's view-distance bounds,
then applies the existing centre-distance and frustum tests. Its opaque/translucent lists use
reusable primitive arrays, keep the original z/y/x draw order, and update occupancy when a chunk
is uploaded or removed. Static chunk matrices only update their three translation components;
they no longer repeat identity/rotation/scale work per draw.

Each meshing worker owns one reusable `VoxelMesher.ChunkSnapshot`. It copies contiguous rows
from the authoritative volume under a single lock and captures the chunk version with those
bytes. Both material passes use this same padded snapshot. A pass with no relevant material in
the core **or neighbour halo** returns shared empty mesh data before entering JNI; the halo is
important because native boundary slices can emit neighbouring faces. Padding is cleared before
reuse to keep clipped map edges correct. Native vertex, index, material and AO output is checked
against the previous meshing path on a deterministic Alpine sample.

GL uploads retain the six-chunk/96-result frame limits and additionally use a soft 2 MiB/2 ms
budget between uploads. The first nonempty result is always admitted, even if oversized, so no
chunk can starve. This cannot interrupt a driver call already in progress. Unchanged voxel
textures bypass `VoxelGpuScene.updateEdits` before issuing any texture-binding calls.

`PerformanceInstrumentedTest` runs alternating, warmed A/B workloads with five-run medians on
the Android device. Meshing uses 256 deterministic Alpine chunks, both passes; culling uses
the 40×3×36 battlefield grid at a 240-voxel radius. These measure subsystem CPU cost, not end-to-end
FPS or complete map loading time, and intentionally impose no device-dependent timing assertions.

## Simulation

### Arena movement and timed training

`ArenaMovement` owns allocation-free horizontal acceleration, capped landing momentum and the
slide state; `Match` retains gravity, jump requests, stance and AABB collision. With Bhop off,
ordinary walking and airborne horizontal movement follow the current input immediately, including
release while descending a one-block ledge. With Bhop on, ordinary air strafe preserves momentum
when the movement stick/keys are released. The HUD speed line is visible only with Bhop on;
movement-course timers and instructions remain available with either setting.
The **Bhop / Slide hop** match modifier requires Jump → release Jump → hold crouch in the air
→ land → press Jump within 0.18 seconds. The fresh airborne crouch edge may occur on ascent or
descent; it remains armed until landing while crouch is held. Landing itself grants no bonus.
Only a successful, headroom-checked follow-up jump gains 14% of entry speed plus 10% of the
current sprint reference. A fresh jump press can be buffered up to 0.10 seconds before landing.
Release and re-press the crouch/slide action for each hop. Jumping from the slide works with
that action still held, unless a ceiling prevents standing. Holding Jump no longer repeats
grounded jumps. Holding crouch across hops or aiming cannot arm a fresh combo. Missing the
landing window causes gradual momentum loss, not an immediate velocity snap. Bhop speed is capped at 2.5 times the current
weapon/class sprint reference; Double Speed scales that reference. Ordinary movement retains
the 1.75-times cap. Neither air strafe nor the slide-hop controller allocates per frame.

An armed airborne combo, a successful hop's flight and its valid landing window use direct
bhop steering: the camera-relative movement direction replaces the horizontal velocity's
heading each fixed tick, retaining its magnitude. Turning the camera while moving forward,
strafing or reversing therefore does not leave drift along the old axis. The landing slide
also follows this heading; neither steering nor an analog stick's magnitude awards speed.
With no movement input, the last heading and airborne speed are retained. Once the landing
window is missed, aiming begins or the movement state is reset, direct steering ends and
ordinary momentum/air strafe rules apply again. Disabling Bhop restores immediate ordinary
horizontal input. Releasing crouch
after a successful hop does not cancel its airborne steering.

Without the Bhop modifier, press crouch while already sprinting at at least 78% of sprint speed to slide. The slide keeps its
entry direction, adds a capped 16% initial boost and decelerates for up to 0.75 seconds. Releasing
crouch, becoming airborne or aiming cancels it. A 1.1-second cooldown and a new crouch press are
required for another ordinary slide. Default keyboard controls are Shift (sprint; landing
slide/crouch in Bhop), Ctrl (crouch), Space
(jump); touch sprints by pushing the left stick fully forward, then uses the crouch/jump HUD
buttons. Bindings remain configurable.

Automatic voxel step-up retains immediate collision resolution while a bounded negative camera
offset eases the view upward across multiple ticks. A diagonal corner may resolve one step on
each horizontal axis. The combined eye height stays inside the player's collision body. Real
auto-jumps retain their ballistic trajectory, with smoothed weapon follow-through and a light
landing response; scoped view models suppress the extra weapon offset. Spawn, course restart
and death reset this presentation state. View motion advances before firing; the camera and
local hitscan, penetration, melee, projectile and muzzle origins share the bounded eye height
so stair smoothing cannot separate the visible crosshair from the attack ray. Bots retain their
physical eye height.
Keyboard bhop resolves Jump, Crouch and the Sprint slide alias through `KeyBindings`, never by
checking literal Shift/Space codes in the movement controller. The training HUD caches a hint
with those configured key names when the match starts; touch mode uses action names instead.
The HUD displays horizontal metres per second and SLIDE/BHOP status without new per-frame strings.

**Movement Training** is a bot-free, unlimited mode with a fixed procedural `MovementCourse`, a
knife-only loadout and seven ordered gates described by `CourseLayout`. Cross the green start
gate to start timing, jump through the designated blue gate, slide through the orange gate, and
cross the yellow finish. Return to the green start pad to reset for another attempt. Pausing
freezes the timer. `MovementTrial` uses swept gate crossings, validates direction/width/height
and stance, and invalidates runs on death or large teleports. It is a local training timer, not
an anti-cheat or an online leaderboard.

`MovementRecords` stores personal best milliseconds on the UI thread in SharedPreferences,
independently for each sanitized modifier mask and course version. The render thread only
publishes primitive results in `HudSnapshot`. Geometry, physics/complete runs, pause timing,
speed caps and record persistence have dedicated unit/instrumentation tests.

### Additional match modifiers

The existing dynamic modifier list now includes eight more stable bits (512 through 65536):

- **No Fall Damage:** bypasses landing-impact damage, not fatal falls into the void.
- **Double Jump:** one additional airborne jump per landing/respawn. It needs a fresh jump press;
  holding Bhop never consumes it. Bot obstacle-hop requests share `JumpRules` and the same budget.
- **Vampire:** restores 25% of actual enemy health removed, clamped to maximum health. Armour,
  lethal overkill, self-damage, friendly fire and dead attackers cannot generate healing.
- **Infinite Ammo:** reserve is inexhaustible but magazines empty and timed reloads still occur.
  Applies to purchased/respawned guns and thrown equipment, including a reserve already at zero.
- **No Reload:** magazine rounds are not consumed and reload requests do nothing. Fire cadence,
  semi-auto trigger resets, burst state, recoil and accuracy bloom remain intact. It can coexist
  with Infinite Ammo; the HUD shows infinity for the appropriate count.
- **Explosive Death:** queues a five-metre death blast (100 damage at the centre, quadratic falloff).
  Solid cover and team eligibility are respected. Each dead body owns its blast/kill credit;
  bounded iterative chain reactions use captured positions, never recursive calls. Headshots Only
  still rejects non-headshot damage, including these blasts. After combat ends, blasts are visual.
- **Mirror Loadout:** all initial spawns use independent weapons cloned from the human's starting
  kit, including Battle Field's selected class gun. Purchases/progression after spawn remain free
  to diverge. Disabled for zombie, knife-only and Gun Game rules; mutually exclusive with Random
  Weapons, with Mirror taking precedence when sanitizing a saved conflicting mask.
- **Hardcore HUD:** hides the minimap, silhouettes/highlights, directional damage hints, kill feed,
  weapon-name/free-arsenal hints and training instructions. Health/ammo, score, objective actions,
  timers and touch controls remain usable. It does not enable Battlefield Hardcore's friendly
  fire or change regeneration. The HUD and GPU consume the same match-controlled visibility policy.

`Match` owns the world, the combatants, projectiles, effects and the mode rules, and runs at a
fixed 60 Hz step driven from the render thread. It publishes a double-buffered `HudSnapshot` that
the UI thread reads without locking.

- **Ballistics** — per-pellet cone spread from stance, movement and accumulated bloom; AABB hit
  probe with head, body and leg regions; distance falloff; one wall-penetration step for weapons
  that support it.
- **Bots** — `BotBrain` is a four-state machine (patrol, engage, hunt, regroup) with field of view
  and line-of-sight checks, gunfire hearing, a reaction delay, aim error that decays while
  tracking, strafing, grenade usage and stuck detection. `Difficulty` scales all of it.
  Battlefield squads divide between assault, defense and early side-road flanks, with each
  member completing its own approach. Marksmen use cached overwatch positions behind their
  living infantry, keep that role with a pistol, brake to settle shots and do not chase lost
  contacts or bunny-hop into an assault. Flankers, supporters and defenders preserve their
  assignments during firefights. Both teams receive the same mix of rifle personalities.
  Squad planning runs every 1.2 seconds; support and perch selection share a 24-ray budget.
- **Navigation** — `NavGrid` stores every standable level per column, not just the top surface, so
  roofs, balconies, catwalks and doorways under overhangs all path correctly. `PathFinder` is a
  weighted A* over that graph with preallocated arrays and a binary heap.
- **Modes** — team deathmatch (kill limit), deathmatch (kill limit), gun game (twelve-step ladder),
  zombie survival (one life, escalating waves), knife fight (blades only, one hit kills).

### Weapons are taken in the round, never in the menu

There is no loadout screen. Every gun in the game is handed out by the buy menu inside a match, and
the menu's coins buy none of them — they accumulate for skins, which do not exist yet. The two
deathmatch modes and the economy modes reach the same list by different rules.

**Deathmatch and team deathmatch have no economy at all.** They declare a free arsenal: every
weapon costs nothing, the list never closes, and you can swap as often as you like from the first
second of the round. You spawn holding a rifle rather than a pistol. This is deliberate — the two
casual modes are meant to be unbalanced, and the buy menu there is really an inventory.

### Round money and the buy menu

Zombie survival is the mode that still runs an economy. It hands the player round money — 1600 at the opening countdown — and pay it out per kill:
900 for a knife, 550 for a shotgun, 450 for an SMG, 400 for a grenade, 350 for a pistol, 300 for a
rifle or sniper, 250 for a machine gun, plus 50 for a headshot, and 350 on death so a losing player
is never stranded. It buys nothing outside the match and is thrown away with it.

You spawn with a pistol, a knife, a grenade and an **empty primary slot**. A
rifle costs 2700 against an opening wallet of 1600, so the first life is deliberately a pistol life.
Buying is open during the countdown, for twenty seconds after each spawn, and for as long as you are
dead — the HUD shows the wallet and the seconds left, and a BUY button appears whenever the window
is actually open.

A purchase writes into the combatant's **kit** as well as into its hands, and every respawn re-issues
that kit fresh. So a gun bought once stays bought for the rest of the match, and you never respawn
holding the empty magazine you died with. Buying a weapon you already carry is refused rather than
charged twice.

The UI thread never touches the simulation: the buy dialog posts `WeaponType` ordinals into the
`InputState` mailbox, next to the jump and reload latches, and the match consumes them at the top of
its player update — before the pause check, since the dialog is modal and the player is always
"paused" while spending. Gun game and knife fight declare no buy menu; their weapons are the mode.
Enter and numpad Enter close the modal buy dialog on key release. The dialog consumes key-down,
repeats and key-up so closing cannot also buy the focused weapon or fire after returning.

The persistent coins are unrelated. They accumulate across matches and buy no weapons at all; the
menu screen that used to be the loadout is now the skins screen, and shows the coin balance waiting
for something to spend it on.

### Knife fight

A free-for-all to twenty kills where the mode, not the loadout, decides the weapon: every combatant
is stripped to a single knife on spawn and again on every respawn, so no gun can reappear
mid-match. Bots need no special casing — their engage state already closes to melee range for a
melee weapon, which is the same path zombie survival uses.

The Combat Knife itself is the lethal one, everywhere and not only here: 150 damage against 100 HP,
so any connected swing kills regardless of where it lands. A backstab is no longer the way to kill
in one hit; it just stops mattering. That applies to the melee slot in every mode and to the quick
melee bash.

The horde is the one exception. Zombies swing a mode-only `SCRAP_KNIFE` that keeps the old two-hit
numbers and the older, plainer model, because a wave that killed on contact would end zombie
survival on the first spawn.

### Aiming is measured from each weapon's own sights

Every gun is modelled around its grip and pivots there, so a single shared aim-down-sights offset
put the receiver in front of the eye instead of the sights — on the longer guns you were, in effect,
aiming with the handle. `Models.sightLift` measures the top of each model ahead of the grip — the
front sight on an iron sighted gun, the scope tube on a sniper — and the view model drops by exactly
that, half a voxel short so the eye looks just over the metal rather than through it. It ranges from
3.5 voxels on the shotgun to 6.5 on the sniper and the launcher, against the 2.3 everything used to
share.

### The view model carries its own motion

The weapon in your hands is drawn with its own previous local transform, not just the previous
camera. Without that, sway, recoil and above all a weapon swap wrote zero velocity, and the
temporal passes happily reprojected the gun you had just put away onto the one you had drawn — two
weapons overlapping in the same hand. A swap now also drops the model out of frame and raises the
new one over the switch timer, and the history is dropped outright when the model itself changes,
since the previous frame holds nothing that geometry can reproject onto.

### Sniper optics

Scoped weapons do not merely narrow the field of view. As the optic comes up, `Match` damps a
scope blend that three things read at once: the camera zooms to the weapon's magnification, the
view model is dropped, and the HUD paints the sight — everything outside a circular lens goes
black, with a mil-dot reticle inside it. The tone mapper darkens the image toward the lens edge
over the same blend, so the rendered picture and the painted surround meet without a seam. Hit
feedback still shows through the optic.

## War economy

`sim/` is a second simulation, independent of the shooter and of Android: one country under
invasion by a stronger neighbour, and the firms inside it deciding whether to stay, move inland or
leave. It exists to be measured, not played — the shooter's frame loop is only one of the ways to
drive it.

The clock is the load-bearing part. `WarSim.advance(deltaSeconds, speed)` never lets frame time
reach the model: it accumulates real seconds and runs whole ticks of `SimConfig.secondsPerTick`.
Speed changes how many ticks pass per second, never what a tick does, so a run watched at 1× and
the same run fast-forwarded at 20× produce byte-identical output, and `runTicks` gives the same
numbers again with no clock at all. Every draw comes from one seeded `Random`, so a run is
reproducible from `SimConfig.seed` alone.

One tick, in order: produce, tax and spend, move the front, let firms decide, advance moves in
progress, apply damage, move labour, then measure. `SimMetrics` is the row that falls out, `SimLog`
turns a run into CSV, and `SimBatch` runs a `Policy` across many seeds headless — a hundred runs is
seconds, which is what makes the model usable as evidence rather than as an anecdote.

Firms value three futures over the same horizon — stay, move inland, go abroad — discounting output
by a per-tick hazard built from the region's status and its distance to the front, and paying a
capital cost to move that their sector's mobility sets. A mine's mobility is zero and it never
moves at any price; an IT firm's is near one. Beliefs about danger lag reality on purpose: the firms
that read the front late are where the losses come from.

Regions may have an `EconomicZone`, and `EconomicZone.NONE` is a first-class case rather than a
missing value: it is the plain region every zone effect is measured against, and most of the default
map has it. Zones can be created and revoked at runtime, which makes "declare a zone here" a policy
lever like any other.

## Exteriors

A voxel world has no textures, so a building only reads as a building if the detail is made of
voxels. The built-up maps are put together from exterior primitives rather than painted boxes:

- **Brick courses.** `brickFill` lays two tones alternating along the course, with every other
  course offset half a brick so the joints stagger the way real brickwork does. Three liveries —
  white, orange and tan — each with their own darker tone.
- **`building()`** replaces the old `house()`: brick walls, a plinth at the base, a painted band
  under the eaves, plaster quoins on all four corners, a framed door, framed windows on three
  sides, and a flat roof behind a parapet with a tiled lip.
- **Framed openings.** A door or window is an opening plus a light frame ring and, for a door, a
  dark recess above head height, so it is a door rather than a hole punched in a wall.
- **Paving.** `tiledFloor` runs grout lines on a grid instead of a checkerboard, `kerb` edges a
  planted corner, `deck` lays plank strips in two wood tones.
- **Props.** `oilDrum` is the two tone drum with steel ribs, `panelCrate` the big grey container
  with seams down every edge and a cross on each face, `pool` a sunken basin with a white rim and
  translucent water you can see into.
- **Perimeter.** The boundary wall is brickwork with a painted coping along the top, since it is
  the surface you spend the whole match looking at.

None of it costs anything at run time: the greedy mesher merges by material, so a two tone wall is
a few more quads, not another draw call. `MapExteriorTest` builds each town map and asserts the
palette is actually present — brickwork in both tones, painted trim, paved ground, drums and
crates — because this is exactly the kind of detail a refactor loses silently.

### Authored places and render-only scenery

`BattlefieldMaps` builds each 640×64×576 battlefield as one authored landscape. It no longer
copies a 192×192 district across the map. City combines an old town, enterable supermarket,
circular civic plaza, library, fire station, transit depot and lakeside park. Farmland combines
a circular market village, orchard, grain farm, meandering canal, five bridges and windmill.
Alps uses branching mountain ridges around a resort village, hotel, gorge footbridge,
gondola station and frozen lake; hiking and ski paths follow the graded terrain. Each landscape
owns its cover and preserves the navigation spines, five capture areas and fifty deployments
per team. Alpine grading bounds adjacent terrain rises to one voxel before structures are placed.

`map.scenery.MapDressing` routes village, urban, alpine and industrial maps to theme-specific
authoring classes; Farmland adds its scenery during landscape generation. Large walkable
structures remain in `VoxelWorld`, including supermarket shelves, multilevel apartment stairs,
the alpine bridge deck and hotel balcony, station concourse, and lake boardwalk.

`MapScenery` owns separate, build-time `DecorBox` and `Landmark` lists. `DecorRecipes` generates
flowers, picket fences, striped awnings, vents, square pipes, cables and 3x5-pixel lettering using
only cuboids and bit masks. These details do **not** enter collision, navigation, bullet raycasts
or the authoritative voxel volume. They are visual, non-destructible set dressing; a slender
decorative fence is not gameplay cover. Rebuilding a map clears both lists.

`DecorationMesher` creates direct native-order buffers at load time, grouping palette materials
in 32-metre X/Z cells. Each cuboid has six maximal faces, with winding tested against outward
normals. Batch AABBs include boxes that cross cell boundaries. `DecorationRenderer` uploads one
VAO/VBO/IBO per cell, then applies frustum and distance culling without frame-time mesh generation
or temporary objects. Details use the existing G-buffer, surface palette, lighting and motion
vectors. Voxel landmarks cast traced shadows; visual-only details receive lighting but are not
independent blockers in the voxel ray-tracing acceleration structure.

Dressed maps have named callout zones, with eight in Battlefield City and Alps and nine in
Farmland. `MapPreview` shows numbered pins and a three-column legend that grows with the number
of places; the menu also lists the names. The HUD uses the nearest callout within its authored
radius, reusing a stored String reference. Hardcore HUD and killcam hide this extra location label.
Village uses Market / Bell Tower / Blue House; Warehouse highlights Container Hall / Control
Office / Catwalk; Facility highlights Laboratory / Server Hall / Reactor.

`SceneryTest` covers winding, buffer format, batching, bounds, culling, opaque palettes, duplicate
builds, resolved spawns, bridge headroom, intact gorge bedrock, lake ice and forest clusters.
`BattlefieldMapsTest` checks all strategic route cores, capture centres, distinct district
signatures and actual navigation paths through supermarket, farm shop, hotel, station and bridge.
`tools/PreviewBattlefields.java` exports reproducible overhead and isometric geometry previews
from compiled app classes without Android.
`RendererInstrumentedTest` draws a decor cube into a real GLES 3 G-buffer, reads back its material
and AO channels, and repeats after GL context recreation. `SceneryInstrumentedTest` loads actual
dressed maps, verifies HUD locations and saves reproducible screenshots in the app cache for QA.

## Playful maps

The bright maps need a few things the competitive ones do not:

- **A real void.** Nothing is solid below `y = 0`, so stepping off a floating island drops you out
  of the world; the match loop turns that fall into a death credited to the world rather than a
  silent teleport. Sky Islands is built on this — nine islands at one shared height with a raised
  plateau in the middle, tied together by rope bridges whose spans are measured against the island
  radii so both ends land on solid ground.
Crouch is a toggle, and jump is how you get out of it: pressing jump while crouched stands the
player up and releases the toggle rather than hopping, so the button and the stance never disagree.

- **Jump pads.** A material the match loop reads: stand on it while grounded and you are launched.
  They carry the vertical routes on Sky Islands and shortcut onto the battlements in Candy Castle.
- **A saturated palette.** Sixteen bright materials — candy pink, mint, sky blue, sunny yellow,
  grape, coral, bright grass, light wood, hay, painted roofs, lanterns — alongside the muted set.
- **Shape primitives** for the storybook silhouettes: cones for turret roofs, discs, rounded
  floating islands, plank bridges with rope posts, hanging banners and palms.

Spawns resolve to the first level a body actually fits on rather than to the top of the column,
which is what keeps a spawn placed inside a house on its floor instead of on its roof.

## Touch controls

Every finger keeps the role it was given when it went down. A button belongs to its finger until
that finger lifts, and once the finger slides past a small dead zone it also steers the camera —
so the trigger stays held while you turn. Any additional finger that is not the movement stick
looks as well, and several may be down at once.

The aim button latches by default: tap it to come up behind the sight and tap again to drop out,
rather than having to keep a thumb on it. The latch is cleared on death, on pause and at the buy
menu, so a respawn never starts scoped. Holding to aim is a switch away in Settings → Controls.

Aiming does not pull the trigger. "Auto fire on aim" still exists for anyone who wants the old
behaviour, but it is off by default — bringing the sight up should only bring the sight up. Its
preference key is versioned (`auto_fire_v2`): earlier builds defaulted it to true and wrote that to
disk, so changing the code default alone would have left every existing install still auto firing.

`HudLayout` persists a global size, an opacity, and a per-control size and offset for each of the
ten action buttons plus the movement stick. Offsets are stored as a fraction of the screen, so a
layout arranged on one device lands correctly on another.

The arrange screen — reachable from the main menu and from Settings → Buttons & layout — drops the
same `HudView` into edit mode: drag any control (the stick included) to move it, tap one to point
the per-control size slider at it, and reset either that control or the whole layout. The pause
menu carries the global size and opacity sliders alongside volume, sensitivity and the graphics
preset, so the HUD can also be adjusted mid-match.

## Keyboard and mouse

The game plays on a physical keyboard and mouse as well as by touch — Chromebooks, DeX, tablets
with a keyboard case, emulators. `DeviceInput` turns key and pointer events into the same
`InputState` the HUD writes to: the four movement keys are combined into a unit vector (so
diagonals are not faster), one-shot actions ignore auto-repeat, and mouse look prefers pointer
capture, which gives relative motion with no cursor to run into the screen edge. Without capture
it falls back to hover deltas.

Touch and device input never fight: `InputState` keeps both sources separately and merges them —
keyboard movement takes priority while a direction key is held and hands back to the stick when
released, and fire or aim stays held while *either* source holds it. The on-screen controls hide
the moment a key or the mouse is used and come back on the next real touch.

`KeyBindings` persists a key code per action and is edited in Settings → Keyboard & mouse: tap a
row, press a key, done. Binding a key clears it from whatever else held it, so two actions can
never share one. Navigation and hardware keys are refused, so back, home and volume always stay
with the system. The same screen carries mouse sensitivity and an invert-vertical switch.

## Native layer

Audio startup never synthesises PCM or opens/closes an `AudioTrack` on the UI thread.
`audio.SoundBank` prepares one process-wide cache of 58 sound types and three variants on a
background worker, publishing the UI clip first, then one clip per sound, then extra variants.
Playback temporarily uses variant zero when another variant is not ready. Menu, match and
resume callbacks reuse the same immutable clips. Each `AudioEngine` session owns its track and
mix buffers; a generation token cancels obsolete sessions without joining their workers from
Activity lifecycle callbacks. Track creation, streaming and release happen on the audio worker.

`libvoxel_native.so` exposes two JNI facades:

- `voxel.NativeBridge` — greedy meshing with per-corner ambient occlusion, the owning copy of the
  volume, ray casts, line of sight, box collision, the brick distance field and the height map.
- `audio.NativeSynth` — the procedural sound bank: gunshots, explosions, footsteps, mechanical
  clicks, hit markers and UI blips, all built from noise, filters and envelopes.

Both have pure-Java fallbacks so the game still runs — and the unit tests still pass — when the
shared library cannot be loaded.

- 203 JVM unit tests: palette, volume traversal, meshing, weapon timing and modifiers, map validity,
  scenery, arena movement, navigation reachability on every map and headless simulations.
- All twelve shader programs are compiled offline with the NDK's `glslc` as part of the workflow

Release signing is injected from the environment, without secrets in source:
